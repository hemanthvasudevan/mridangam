Buyer's Guide · India

Finding a mridangam worth its price

A good mridangam is a long-term instrument — often played for decades with occasional re-tuning and re-skinning. Buying one deserves the same care as buying any fine handmade instrument.

Before you pay

What to check on any mridangam

  • Wood quality: The shell should be a single, well-seasoned piece — jackwood is standard — with no visible cracks, warping, or repaired splits along the barrel.
  • Head tension & tone: Both heads should produce a clear, ringing tone with a single stroke, not a dull or buzzing thud. A properly built syahi patch gives the treble head a definite, singable pitch.
  • Even skin, no wrinkles: Look closely at both drumheads for uneven stretching, loose patches, or wrinkling near the rim — signs of a rushed or amateur build.
  • Lacing quality: The leather straps should be tight, evenly spaced, and free of fraying. Loose or mismatched lacing makes an instrument hard to tune and keep in tune.
  • Size for the player: Mridangams commonly range from about 18 to 24 inches long. Smaller sizes suit lighter, higher-pitched accompaniment (often paired with female vocalists); larger sizes suit deeper concert pitches. A teacher's recommendation is the most reliable guide.
  • Weight & portability: A traditional wooden-shelled mridangam typically weighs somewhere around 8–12 kilograms. If frequent travel matters, ask specifically about fibreglass or lighter-shell options.
  • After-sales support: Because skins settle and stretch with use, ask whether the maker offers re-tuning, re-skinning, or repair support after purchase — this matters more than it seems at first.
  • A sound demo before shipping: Reputable makers and sellers are usually willing to send a short video or voice-call demo of the actual instrument being played before you commit, especially for long-distance or international orders.
Where mridangams are traditionally made

The instrument's own geography

Thanjavur The historic heartland

Long associated with Carnatic music itself; home to several established mridangam-making families and workshops.

Chennai Concentration of makers & buyers

The city's concert culture supports a cluster of long-standing instrument shops and individual makers.

Kumbakonam Traditional craft town

A smaller but well-regarded center for percussion-instrument making in the Thanjavur region.

Salem Additional making hub

Home to further workshops supplying mridangams and related percussion instruments across South India.

One more thing

Budget realistically

Entry-level, student-grade mridangams in India commonly start in the low thousands of rupees, while concert-grade instruments from established makers cost considerably more, reflecting better wood, more careful skin work, and a properly built syahi patch. As with most handmade instruments, the price difference is almost always audible.
