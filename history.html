Origins & Evolution

A drum older than the concert stage

Long before it accompanied a modern kutcheri, the mridangam's ancestors were carved into temple stone and named in Sanskrit treatises on dramatic art.

A timeline

From temple courtyards to the concert hall

Ancient period

Early mentions and mythology

Barrel drums closely related to the mridangam appear in ancient Sanskrit performance texts such as the Natya Shastra, and in early Tamil literary tradition. In popular mythology, the drum is linked to Nandi, the bull-vehicle of Shiva, who is said to have kept rhythm for Shiva's cosmic dance — giving the instrument its old epithet as a "divine instrument."

Medieval period · Chola era

Carved into stone

Temple sculpture from the Chola dynasty, including reliefs at the Brihadeeswarar Temple in Thanjavur (completed 1010 CE), depict drummers and dancers together — physical evidence that barrel-drum percussion was already integral to South Indian temple performance nearly a thousand years ago.

17th–18th century · Thanjavur court

Refined under royal patronage

The Thanjavur Maratha court became a major center of South Indian classical music, the same environment that produced the Carnatic Trinity of composers — Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar, and Syama Sastri. Percussion accompaniment matured alongside this compositional flowering, developing more formal conventions for supporting a soloist.

19th–20th century

Two schools take shape

Distinct lineages, or banis, crystallized around master teachers — most notably the school associated with Tanjore Vaidyanatha Iyer, and the Pudukkottai school associated with Manpoondia Pillai. Their students, most famously Palghat Mani Iyer and Palani Subramania Pillai, would go on to define 20th-century mridangam artistry and formalize the modern tani avartanam tradition.

Mid-to-late 20th century

A golden age of exponents

Concert culture built around organizations like the Madras Music Academy elevated percussionists to headline status for the first time, with awards such as the Sangeetha Kalanidhi recognizing mridangam artists alongside vocalists for the first time.

Present day

Global stage, ongoing innovation

The mridangam now travels with the Carnatic diaspora worldwide and has crossed into fusion and world-music collaborations. Makers and players continue to experiment — from fibreglass shells to mechanically tunable hardware — while the core craft of skin, wood, and hand-tied lacing remains largely unchanged.

Continuity

An oral tradition, still

Despite centuries of change, the instrument is still overwhelmingly taught the way it always has been: through gurukula-style apprenticeship, where a student learns by sitting beside a teacher for years, absorbing rhythm before technique and technique before performance. That living transmission — more than any single historical document — is what has carried the mridangam from temple courtyard to the modern stage.
