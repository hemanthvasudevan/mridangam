Carnatic Percussion · South India

The heartbeat of Carnatic music

The mridangam is a double-headed, barrel-shaped drum from Tamil Nadu — the primary rhythmic voice of South Indian classical music, laid across the player's lap and spoken to with the fingers of both hands.

At a glance

Quick facts

Origin Tamil Nadu, South India

Rooted in the temple and court traditions of the Tamil-speaking south, now played wherever Carnatic music travels.

Classification Double-headed membranophone

A hollowed wooden barrel closed at both ends with tensioned animal-skin heads.

Playing position Horizontal, on the lap

Struck with the fingers and palms of both hands — no sticks are used.

Instrument family Cousin to the pakhawaj & khol

Part of a wider South Asian family of barrel drums, each shaped by its own regional repertoire.

Two heads, one voice

A treble side and a bass side

Every mridangam has two unequal faces joined by a single wooden shell. The smaller valantalai (right head) carries a permanent black tuning patch and speaks in clear, pitched syllables. The larger thoppi (left head) has no fixed patch — the player dabs on a temporary paste of flour and water before playing to draw out a deep, rounded bass.

Together the two heads let a single player produce a full rhythmic vocabulary: crisp treble strokes for intricate patterns, and a resonant low end that anchors the pulse — all phrased in the spoken syllables (sollus) that every mridangam student learns to recite before ever touching the drum.

Where you'll hear it

Everywhere Carnatic music is performed

The mridangam accompanies vocal and instrumental concerts, Bharatanatyam recitals, and temple festival music across South India and its global diaspora. It rarely leads a piece from silence — it enters in dialogue, tracking the singer's or instrumentalist's phrasing, then steps forward on its own during the percussion interlude known as the tani avartanam.

Vocal & instrumental concerts

The core accompanying instrument in nearly every Carnatic kutcheri, alongside the violin.

Bharatanatyam & dance

Drives the rhythmic footwork (nritta) passages of classical South Indian dance.

Temple & festival music

Present in temple ensembles and devotional music traditions across South India.
