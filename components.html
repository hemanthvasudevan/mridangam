Construction & Craft

One shell, two skins, weeks of hand craft

A mridangam looks simple from a distance — a wooden barrel with two drumheads. Up close, it is a precise assembly of wood, hide, paste, and hand-laced leather, each part tuned to work with the others.

The parts

Anatomy of the instrument

01

Shell (kalam)

A single log — traditionally jackwood, prized for its density and resonance — is hollowed into a barrel that is wider at the bass end and narrower at the treble end. Rosewood and, in modern instruments, fibreglass are sometimes used instead.

02

Right head (valantalai)

The smaller, treble head. Made from goat skin, it carries a permanent black tuning patch and produces the drum's clear, pitched syllables.

03

Left head (thoppi)

The larger, bass head, usually of buffalo or cow hide. It has no permanent patch — its deep tone is coaxed out fresh at every sitting.

04

Tuning patch (syahi / karana)

A dense black disc built up in dozens of thin layers from cooked rice or tamarind paste and iron or manganese powder, then burnished smooth. This permanent patch is what gives the right head its definite, bell-like pitch.

05

Temporary paste (maavu)

A wet dough of wheat flour or semolina, pressed onto the bass head just before playing to lower its pitch and thicken its tone — then wiped away afterward so the skin can breathe.

06

Hoops (kaani)

Circular rings of plaited leather sit under the rim of each head, giving the skins something to be laced against and helping distribute tension evenly.

07

Lacing straps

Long leather thongs run diagonally between the two hoops around the length of the shell, forming the crisscross pattern that visually — and structurally — holds the whole drum together.

08

Tuning blocks (gaddi)

Small, movable wooden cylinders wedged under the lacing. Tapping them up or down the shell tightens or loosens specific straps, letting a player fine-tune pitch by ear, often against a tambura or electronic shruti box.

09

Rim weight (optional)

Some players add a small dab of paste or a weighted ring near the edge of the treble head for a final, subtle pitch adjustment before a performance.

From log to instrument

How one is actually built

  1. Seasoning & turning: A jackwood log is dried for months, then turned on a lathe into the hollow barrel shape, wider at one end.
  2. Preparing the skins: Goat and buffalo hides are cleaned, limed, and stretched to the right thickness for each head.
  3. Building the syahi: On the treble head, the black tuning patch is applied in many thin coats over several days, each layer dried and burnished before the next is added.
  4. Mounting the heads: Both skins are stretched over their hoops and fixed to the shell.
  5. Lacing: Leather straps are threaded diagonally between the two hoops, drawing the heads to the correct tension.
  6. Final tuning: Tuning blocks are tapped into position and the maker plays the drum against a reference pitch until both heads speak cleanly together.
A note on materials

Why these particular materials

Jackwood is dense enough to project sound without cracking under the tension of the lacing. Goat skin, being thin and elastic, takes a crisp, high-pitched voice well, while thicker buffalo or cow hide handles the low-frequency bass response the left head needs. The syahi's iron or manganese content is what gives the treble head its unusually definite, singable pitch — a feature that sets barrel drums like the mridangam apart from most other hand drums worldwide.
